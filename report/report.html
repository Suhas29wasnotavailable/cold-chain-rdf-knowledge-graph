1. Problem, purpose and scope

A vaccine distributor moves shipments between its warehouses in refrigerated containers. Each container carries a temperature sensor that logs readings during the trip. Today these records live in separate systems: a shipment sheet, the sensor logger's export, and product data sheets that state the permitted temperature range. Answering a simple question such as "Did shipment S101 stay within its required range while it was in transit?" means joining all three by hand.

Purpose. Describe shipments, containers, sensors, readings, products, warehouses, carriers and dispatch/arrival events as one machine-readable RDF graph with a small RDFS vocabulary, so the question above can be answered with a single query and the data can be exchanged with other systems.

Scope. In scope: the case entities (shipments S101–S102, containers C7–C8, sensors T91–T92, products Vaccine-A/B, warehouses Bengaluru-North and Mysuru-Central, dispatch and arrival events, timed readings, permitted ranges, carrier and responsible warehouse). I added realistic fictional details: one distributor (MediVault), one carrier (FrostLine), one physical lot per shipment with lot number, dose count and expiry date, sensor serial numbers and calibration dates. Out of scope: routing and GPS, people and drivers, regulatory documents, pricing, and any real organisation or medical guidance. All values are fictional.

2. Namespaces and IRIs

The vocabulary and the data live in separate namespaces. Vocabulary terms use cc: <http://example.org/coldchain/vocab#>. Every instance gets a path IRI http://example.org/coldchain/id/<type>/<code>, with one prefix per type, for example shp:S101, ctr:C7, sen:T91, wh:BLR-N, lot:VA-2609-01, evt:S101-dispatch and obs:T91-20261005T1100. IRIs are built only from stable business codes. Names and labels can change without breaking links. The code is also kept as a literal (cc:code "S101") for display and for joining with the source systems. Two external vocabularies are reused: SOSA (W3C Sensor, Observation, Sample and Actuator) and QUDT units (unit:DEG_C). example.org is a placeholder domain. A real deployment would use an IRI space the company controls.

3. RDFS vocabulary

The vocabulary declares 18 classes and 28 properties. Every property has an rdfs:label and a domain and/or range. Literals use typed values: xsd:decimal for temperatures, xsd:dateTime with a +05:30 offset for event and reading times, xsd:date for expiry and calibration dates, and xsd:integer for dose counts.

Instance data

2 organisations, 2 warehouses, 2 vaccines, 2 temperature ranges (+2..+8 °C and −25..−15 °C), 2 lots, 2 containers, 2 sensors, 2 shipments, 4 events and 8 readings. S101 carries Vaccine-A from Bengaluru-North to Mysuru-Central in C7 (sensor T91). S102 carries frozen Vaccine-B from Mysuru-Central to Bengaluru-North in C8 (sensor T92). The data includes two deliberate test cases. T91 records 9.4 °C at 11:00, which is a real excursion during transit. It also records 1.6 °C at 07:30, which is below range but was taken during pre-cooling, before dispatch at 08:00. A correct query must report the first reading and ignore the second.

5. Five key modelling decisions

D1. A product type is separate from the physical lot that is shipped

prod:vaccine-a (a cc:Vaccine) describes what the product is. lot:VA-2609-01 (a cc:ProductLot) is the physical batch in the container, with its lot number, dose count and expiry date. The shipment contains the lot, and the lot points to its product. Alternative rejected: shp:S101 cc:carries prod:vaccine-a. That gives no place for lot-specific facts such as expiry and quantity. It would also attach recall or excursion consequences to the whole product type instead of the affected batch.

D2. The permitted temperature range is its own resource, attached to the product type

rng:2to8C carries cc:minTemperature, cc:maxTemperature and a unit IRI (unit:DEG_C). Ranges are reused: any product with the same storage class points to the same node. The range belongs to the product, not the shipment, because it is a property of the medicine. Shipments inherit it through the lot (Q3). Alternative rejected: two bare literals such as minTemp 2 and maxTemp 8 on each shipment. That copies the rule into every record, loses the unit, and lets copies disagree.

D3. Readings are tied to the sensor, the container and a time, not to the shipment

Each cc:TemperatureObservation has madeBySensor, observedContainer, resultTime and celsiusValue, matching the SOSA pattern. A reading is assigned to a shipment by its time: it counts if it falls between that shipment's dispatch and arrival in the same container (Q4). This matches the real world. The sensor logs readings with no knowledge of shipments, and containers are reused for many trips. The design also lets the model tell the pre-cooling reading (1.6 °C) apart from an excursion during transit (9.4 °C). Alternative rejected: a direct reading → shipment link. It is simpler to query, but it is a judgement stored as fact. It would be wrong for readings outside the trip window, and it would need re-linking every time a container is reused.

D4. Dispatch and arrival are event resources, not date fields on the shipment

evt:S101-dispatch is a cc:DispatchEvent with eventOf, atFacility and eventTime. Each event can therefore record where it happened as well as when. New event types such as handover, customs hold or a second leg can be added as further subclasses of cc:LogisticsEvent without changing the shipment. Alternative rejected: shp:S101 cc:dispatchedAt "2026-10-05T08:00". This needs a new property for every kind of event and cannot say where the event happened.

D5. Derived facts are queried, not stored; external vocabularies are linked by sub-class and sub-property

No excursion flag or "delivered" status is stored. Both follow from the readings, ranges and events, so storing them would duplicate the data and risk contradicting it. They are computed by queries (Q4, Q5). For interoperability, the local terms are declared as specialisations of SOSA (cc:TemperatureObservation ⊑ sosa:Observation, cc:madeBySensor ⊑ sosa:madeBySensor, …). An RDFS reasoner then makes every reading visible to any SOSA-aware tool without changing the data (Q7). Local names were kept because they fit the domain and avoid a full dependency on SOSA.

Interpretation. Q4 is the result that matters most to the business. Answering it combines five sources of facts: the shipment, its lot, the product's range, the dispatch and arrival events, and the container's readings. In the original records these sat in three separate systems. Q7 shows what the RDFS layer adds. The data never states that a reading is a sosa:Observation, but a reasoner concludes it from the vocabulary.

7. Limitations and reflection

  • RDFS does not constrain data. A domain or range does not reject wrong data. It infers new types instead. For example, if a reading were mistakenly linked with cc:eventOf, RDFS would quietly conclude that the reading is a LogisticsEvent. This is why I added the explicit domain/range conformance check and the SPARQL rules in Q8. In production, SHACL shapes would do this job (for example, "exactly one dispatch event per shipment" and "min < max").
  • Open world. A missing reading does not mean the temperature was fine. Q4 can only report excursions among the readings that were recorded. Gaps between readings (C7 has one reading per hour) are not detected. A check on the time between readings would be a useful next query.
  • Time-window attribution assumes one shipment per container at a time. Combined loads would need an explicit container-loading event.
  • One unit only. Readings are stored in °C, and the unit is fixed by the property name celsiusValue. Mixed-unit loggers would need a value-plus-unit node (for example QUDT QuantityValue).
  • Small, fictional dataset. Two shipments are enough to test each query, including one positive and one negative case, but not to test performance or messy real data.
